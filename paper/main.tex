\begin{abstract}
A reasoner is \emph{abstract} if it applies a general procedure to a problem's structure, indifferent to how that structure is concretely instantiated; it is \emph{concrete} if what it learned is tied to the particular instantiation trained on. This provides a testable marker: an abstract reasoner should be able to transfer mastery of one problem instance to another. We study equivalence between problems via alphabet renaming, which can be defined on any symbolic problem, and instantiate it on Sudoku. A transformer trained on one such puzzle transfers to an equivalent one no better than an untrained network, yet a control that copies over its trained parameters instead of retraining recovers its original performance exactly. Reasoning, in that narrow sense, is abstract; recognizing that a new instance calls for it is not. We probe this recognition gap three ways. Freezing the shared network and adapting only a new alphabet's parameters fares worse than training an unfrozen model from scratch on the same data: prior exposure to other alphabets does not ease learning a new one. Recombining already-known symbols into unfamiliar combinations yields graded, partial success: above chance, short of the seamless transfer an abstract reasoner should show, degrading as combinations diverge from training. Replacing the model's fixed, per-alphabet output vocabulary with a mechanism that selects among a puzzle's own visible symbols helps substantially with recombination, but only marginally with transfer to a truly unseen alphabet. These results isolate a specific failure to recognize alphabet-equivalent instances as such, distinct from a general failure to reason, and show several natural interventions narrow, but do not close, this gap.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A model that has learned to solve a problem can be said to reason
\emph{abstractly} about it if the procedure it applies operates on the
problem's structure, indifferent to the particular form in which that
structure is presented; it reasons only \emph{concretely} if what it has
learned is tied to the specific surface form it was trained on, instead
of to the structure underneath \citep{fodor1988connectionism}. This distinction is central to any claim
that a learned system reasons instead of merely fitting patterns in its
training distribution, but such claims are frequently made without a
test that could actually distinguish the two possibilities. Benchmarks
such as SCAN \citep{lake2018generalization} and COGS \citep{kim2020cogs}
test a closely related property, generalizing to unseen
\emph{combinations} of familiar components, and have repeatedly found
large gaps between in-distribution and compositional-generalization
accuracy. We study a different, more basic notion of generalization: not
new combinations of familiar parts, but the exact same combinatorial
structure presented under a purely relabeled vocabulary, asking a
question any account of compositional generalization presupposes an
answer to: does the model recognize the relabeled instance as the
problem it already knows how to solve at all.

We propose a concrete operationalization of this distinction: an
abstract reasoner should treat two different instances of the same
underlying problem interchangeably, since its procedure does not depend
on which particular instantiation it happens to face. We instantiate
``different instances of the same problem'' through the simplest
transform that leaves a problem's structure completely intact while
changing its surface form entirely: renaming its set of symbols.

This gives a general recipe applicable to any symbolic or combinatorial
domain: define equivalence via a checkable relabeling, measure whether a
model trained on one instance transfers, unaided, to an equivalent one,
and use a targeted \emph{weight-transplant} control, copying a trained
model's per-symbol parameters directly onto their counterparts in the
new alphabet, to separate a true failure to generalize from the simpler
confound that the new instance's symbols were never trained at all.

We instantiate this recipe on Sudoku puzzles, whose instances can be
generated inexpensively with a verified unique solution. A transformer
trained on one alphabet transfers to an equivalent puzzle in a different
alphabet at chance level, indistinguishable from an untrained network,
even though the weight-transplant control recovers its original accuracy
\emph{exactly}: the model's procedure reuses across equivalent instances
once told the symbol correspondence, but it never discovers that
correspondence on its own. Probing this recognition gap along three
further axes tells a more nuanced story. Pretraining on several known
alphabets, and an architecture that replaces the model's fixed output
vocabulary with a mechanism selecting among a puzzle's own visible
symbols, each narrow the gap somewhat but leave it open. Scaling up
training-time diversity then resolves it unevenly: recombining symbols
the model already knows becomes essentially solved, while transfer to a
truly novel symbol remains just as broken as at the outset.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item An operationalized, reusable methodology for testing knowledge
  transfer between equivalent instances of a symbolic problem, including
  a weight-transplant control isolating true reasoning-transfer failure
  from never-trained parameters.
  \item Applied to Sudoku, naive transfer across alphabet-equivalent
  puzzles fails completely, while the transplant control recovers full
  performance exactly: the failure is one of recognition, not of
  reusable computation (Section~\ref{sec:naive}).
  \item This recognition gap persists across pretraining diversity and an
  architecture removing a structural bottleneck that prevents zero-shot
  generalization by construction; each narrows but does not close it
  (Sections~\ref{sec:diversity1}--\ref{sec:pointer}).
  \item Training-time data diversity, at much larger scale, resolves the
  gap into two sub-problems: recombining already-known symbols is
  essentially solved, while transfer to a truly novel symbol remains
  unsolved by every intervention tested (Section~\ref{sec:diversity2}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Neural Sudoku solving.} \citet{palm2018recurrent} introduced
\emph{Recurrent Relational Networks}, solving Sudoku via iterative
message passing between cell-level representations on a graph, the
architectural lineage our encoder-based model belongs to.
\citet{wang2019satnet}'s \emph{SATNet} instead embeds a differentiable
MAXSAT solver directly in the network; \citet{chang2020assessing} showed
it fails at visual Sudoku without intermediate labels tying each digit
image to its symbol, a symbol-grounding failure related to the one we
document, though via a different architecture and a different route to
severing the symbol-to-meaning link. That work, and
subsequent neural combinatorial solvers, target solving accuracy within a
single fixed vocabulary; we instead hold that accuracy fixed near ceiling
and ask what happens under a symbol relabeling that leaves the problem
unchanged.

\paragraph{Compositional and systematic generalization.} SCAN
\citep{lake2018generalization} and COGS \citep{kim2020cogs} test whether
sequence models generalize to novel \emph{combinations} of familiar
primitives, both finding persistent, large gaps between in-distribution
and compositional accuracy; \citet{hupkes2020compositionality} formalize
this style of test into a general methodology for probing
compositionality, and \citet{csordas2021devil} show the gap narrows
substantially for Transformers specifically under the right training
details. Our question is more basic: not combining familiar parts in new
ways, only recognizing the exact same combination already trained on,
wearing a different vocabulary. That we still find total failure
suggests the difficulty these benchmarks document may in part reflect an
even more basic failure to abstract away from surface symbol identity at
all.

\paragraph{Symbol binding and variable binding.}
\citet{santoro2017simple}'s \emph{Relation Networks} were an early
architecture for relational reasoning by construction, though over a
fixed input set instead of symbol identity. \citet{webb2021emergent} is
the closest direct precedent for our framing: their \emph{Emergent
Symbol Binding Network} uses an indirection mechanism over external memory
that lets learned rules generalize near-perfectly to entirely novel filler
entities from a handful of examples, because its computation operates on
relations between representations instead of their identity.
\citet{altabaa2024abstractors}'s \emph{Abstractors} formalize a version of
this idea for Transformers directly, via a relational cross-attention
mechanism constrained to depend only on similarity between
representations, never their content. Our Section~\ref{sec:pointer}
pointer-based redesign (predicting a blank cell's value by selecting
among symbols visible elsewhere in the puzzle, a direct application of
Pointer Networks \citep{vinyals2015pointer} and pointer-generator copy
mechanisms \citep{see2017get}) asks a narrower question: whether
removing a fixed, identity-keyed output vocabulary, without also
constraining the encoder's internal computation to be relational, is
enough on its own. Our results (helping substantially with one
sub-problem, only marginally with the other) indicate it is not,
motivating Abstractors' more explicit mechanism as a next step
(Sections~\ref{sec:diversity2}, \ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Symbolic mechanisms in pretrained language models.}
Contemporaneously, \citet{yang2025emergent} identify an emergent symbolic
architecture inside pretrained large language models (symbol
abstraction, symbolic induction, and retrieval heads operating on
abstract variables instead of raw tokens) supporting abstract reasoning
in a related sense, and \citet{feng2024how} similarly find LLMs bind
entities to attributes in context via dedicated binding-identity
representations. Their results establish that such mechanisms can exist inside an
already-trained model; our question is different: which interventions
cause the analogous recognition gap to appear or close, which requires
controlling training itself, in the spirit of work training small
transformers from scratch on algorithmic tasks to study generalization
in isolation \citep{power2022grokking,velickovic2021neural}. We train
small transformers under fully controlled data, so that pretraining
diversity, architecture, and training-time mixing can each be
manipulated one at a time and any resulting behavior change attributed
directly to it.

\section{Problem Setup and Method}
\label{sec:method}

We fix Sudoku terminology used throughout the paper: a
puzzle fixes some cells (the \emph{fixed cells}) of a $9\times9$ grid,
partitioned into nine $3\times3$ boxes, and leaves the rest blank; a
\emph{solution} fills every blank cell so that each of 9 symbols
appears exactly once in every row, column, and box, and we only use
puzzles with a unique solution. Section~\ref{sec:equivalence} below
defines, for combinatorial problems in general, what it means for two
puzzles to be equivalent under a relabeling of their symbols, and the
notation we reuse throughout the paper; the remaining subsections
instantiate the rest of our setup for Sudoku specifically.

\subsection{Equivalence of combinatorial problems}
\label{sec:equivalence}

A combinatorial problem is defined over $n$ roles
subject to some rules; a puzzle is stated using an
\emph{alphabet}: an injective map $A$ from the $n$ canonical roles
$\{1, \ldots, n\}$ to $n$ distinct tokens, together with a fixed token
$0$ reserved as blank and shared by every alphabet (for Sudoku,
$n=9$). For a puzzle $P$ stated in the canonical alphabet (roles
$1, \ldots, n$ used directly as tokens), write $A(P)$ for the puzzle
obtained by relabeling every role $d$ to token $A(d)$, leaving every
other aspect, positions, structure, and rules, unchanged; we call
$A(P)$ ``puzzle $P$ in alphabet $A$.''

This relabeling preserves validity unconditionally: applying the same
substitution everywhere carries any solution of $P$ to a solution of the
correspondingly relabeled puzzle. Two puzzles are \emph{equivalent}
exactly when they are $A(P)$ and $B(P)$ for the same underlying $P$ and
two alphabets $A \neq B$: solving one is the same problem as solving the
other, under a different alphabet.

\subsection{Data}

We generate 200{,}000 puzzles once, in a canonical alphabet (tokens 1 to
9), each with 40 fixed cells and a solution verified unique by
constraint propagation with backtracking, and split them once into a
fixed 90/5/5 train/val/test partition (180{,}000 / 10{,}000 / 10{,}000
puzzles; full generation settings in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams},
Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}). For any other alphabet, we obtain its
puzzle set by applying
that alphabet to this same partition, so every alphabet's split covers
the same underlying puzzles, and comparisons across alphabets are over
\emph{identical} underlying puzzles.

Each alphabet is a disjoint block of 9 token ids: the $k$-th letter (A,
B, C, \ldots) simply shifts every token id by $9(k-1)$, so alphabet A is
the canonical alphabet itself, B shifts every id by $+9$, and so on. We
designate one letter, E, as held out from every model's training data in
every experiment, so it consistently serves as the truly-unseen-symbol
test throughout.

\subsection{Models}

Both architectures we study share an encoder body:
token embeddings, additive row/column/box positional
embeddings, and a standard
pre-norm Transformer encoder \citep{vaswani2017attention} (8 layers,
$d_{\text{model}}=384$, 8 attention heads; full architecture
hyperparameters in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}, Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}).
They differ only in how a blank cell's encoded
representation is turned into a predicted token, illustrated side by
side in Figure~\ref{fig:architectures}.

\paragraph{Classifier head (baseline).} A linear layer maps each cell's
representation to $9K$ logits, one per role per alphabet among the $K$
alphabets ever included in training, and predicts the literal token id
directly (never a ``role within some alphabet,'' which would presuppose
the model already knows its own alphabet's identity). This head's size
is fixed at training time: a novel alphabet's $9$ logit rows do not
exist yet and cannot be added without retraining, which is the
structural bottleneck motivating the pointer head
described next.

\paragraph{Pointer head.} For each blank cell, we instead identify the
(at most 9) distinct token ids that appear among that specific puzzle's
own fixed cells, and predict which of them belongs in the cell. A
learned query, computed from the blank cell's encoded representation, is
compared via dot product against each candidate's raw token-embedding
vector (the same embedding table used on the input side; no separate
output projection exists), and the resulting scores are softmaxed over
the present candidates. This candidate set is always drawn from the
puzzle's own fixed cells, so it never exceeds 9 candidates, unlike the
classifier head's $9K$-sized output, which grows with $K$. A puzzle's
target is only well-defined for a blank cell if the correct token is
itself among that puzzle's own fixed cells, not merely somewhere in the
completed grid; measured directly on our generator, 0.80\% of puzzles
violate this. We filter such puzzles out for training, and deliberately
leave them in for evaluation, so reported numbers reflect this real (if
small) architectural
limitation.

Both architectures optionally support RRN/IREM-style unrolling
\citep{palm2018recurrent,du2022learning} of the shared encoder; all
experiments here use a single pass ($T=1$).

\subsection{The weight-transplant control}
\label{sec:transplant}

Naive transfer between equivalent puzzles $A(P)$ and $B(P)$
(Section~\ref{sec:naive}) cannot tell whether a model's failure to solve
$B(P)$ reflects reasoning that does not generalize across alphabets, or
simply that $B$'s tokens never received a training signal, leaving their
parameters at random initialization regardless of how good the model's
reasoning otherwise is. The weight-transplant control isolates the
second explanation: for the classifier head, we copy, for every role
$d \in \{1, \ldots, 9\}$, $A$'s token-$d$ row of the token embedding
table and of the $\mathrm{Linear}(d_{\text{model}}, 9K)$ output head
(row and bias) directly onto $B$'s token-$d$ row (a plain tensor copy,
no gradient step or retraining) before evaluating on $B$. This tells us
how the model performs on $B$ once it has $B$'s own trained parameters,
without $B$ itself ever appearing during training. The pointer head
needs no analogous control: it never has per-alphabet output parameters
to copy in the first place, which is precisely its motivation.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report two metrics. \emph{Cell accuracy} is the
fraction of blank cells filled with the token recorded in our data.
\emph{Exact match rate} is the fraction of boards where every blank
cell matches.

The weight-transplant control (Section~\ref{sec:transplant}) copies one
alphabet's row onto another's, creating a tie an unrestricted argmax
would break by favoring whichever alphabet has the lower token offset,
an implementation artifact, not anything the model computed. We
therefore restrict the classifier head's argmax to the evaluated
alphabet's own 9 logits (instead of all $9K$) whenever the transplant
control is in play; this does not hand the model information it lacks,
since which alphabet a puzzle belongs to is already visible in its own
fixed cells. The pointer head needs no such fix, its candidate set
already restricted to the puzzle's own tokens by construction.

Because prediction is always restricted to the specific tokens a
puzzle's own fixed cells establish as belonging to it, and every puzzle
has a verified unique solution, exact match rate directly measures
whether the model produced a legal completion (no repeated symbol in any
row, column, or box). Cell accuracy remains a distinct, finer-grained
per-cell measure.

\subsection{Training protocol}

All models are trained with AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019decoupled}, batch
size 256, for 20 epochs unless otherwise noted, on a single A100 GPU per
run. Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} (Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}) lists
the full hyperparameters, including every condition's epoch count and
seed count and the exceptions to these defaults.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:results}

Unless noted otherwise, every experiment below uses the data, model, and
metrics of Section~\ref{sec:method}, trained on a single A100 GPU per
run.

\subsection{Does the model discover the equivalence on its own?}
\label{sec:naive}

The classifier head's disjoint per-alphabet output rows mean it cannot
use a row it never trained; that much is architectural. What is not
settled is how total the resulting failure is, since a shared encoder
processing both alphabets could plausibly leak some above-chance signal
even through untrained output rows. We train the classifier head on
alphabet A only, separately on alphabet B only, and (as a reference
check) on their union, and evaluate each single-alphabet model three
ways: on its own alphabet (\emph{self}); on the other, unaided
(\emph{naive cross}); and on the other with the weight-transplant
control applied (\emph{transplanted cross}).

Table~\ref{tab:naive} shows naive cross-alphabet transfer landing

\emph{self}, but statistically indistinguishable from a network never

to four significant figures, with no retraining. The only thing
separating 11\% from 99\% is knowing the symbol correspondence, not some
deeper representational gap: solving competence is bound to the specific
tokens trained on, and does not spontaneously discover that a renamed
puzzle is the same problem already known how to solve.

\subsection{Does more alphabet diversity help?}
\label{sec:diversity1}

Experiment~\ref{sec:naive} used one alphabet per model. We ask two
further questions with a classifier pretrained on $K=6$ alphabets
(A,B,C,D,F,G; E held out), 60{,}000 puzzles per alphabet, 20 epochs
(99.57\% cell accuracy across all six by the end of training; full
settings in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}, Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}).

\paragraph{Few-shot adaptation.} For held-out E, given $n \in \{100,
1000\}$ labeled E-puzzles (3 seeds each), \emph{finetune} (freezing the
pretrained body, adapting only E's own rows) and \emph{scratch} (a
fresh, unfrozen model) are indistinguishable at $n=100$ (26.8\% vs.\
26.5\% mean cell accuracy); at $n=1000$, scratch clearly wins (69.2\%
vs.\ 39.5\%, finetune nearly flat across seeds). If the frozen body were
a portable, alphabet-agnostic solving procedure, reusing it should have
been easier than relearning everything from scratch; instead it is
entangled with the specific alphabets it was trained on, not a clean,
reusable abstraction (full numbers in Appendix~\ref{app:fewshot}).

\paragraph{Mixed-alphabet compositional test.} Using the same $K=6$
checkpoint, we recombine the 6 known alphabets instead of testing yet
another one: each of the 9 roles independently selects one of the 6
known alphabets to draw its token from, so the same alphabet can, and
typically does, supply more than one role. We call one such
role-to-alphabet assignment a \emph{trial}, evaluated once over the
entire held-out test set. Throughout the paper we reuse the same 12
fixed trials: \emph{control} (all 9 roles from alphabet A),
\emph{maximally mixed} (roles cycle through all 6 alphabets in order),
and 10 independently drawn \emph{random} trials, summarized by range and
mean (full definitions in Appendix~\ref{app:trials}). The resulting
combination was never presented as one coherent alphabet during
training, even though every individual symbol is well learned.

\label{tab:mixed1}

Table~\ref{tab:mixed1} shows accuracy far above the roughly 11\% chance
level but far below the 99\% ceiling, tracking how the trial's 9
role-to-alphabet assignments are distributed: a trial where most roles
draw from just one or two of the 6 alphabets scores high, while one
spread evenly across all 6 scores low. The model has partial, graded
compositional ability instead of a clean, disentangled binding of each
symbol to its meaning.

likely a property of the vector-equality comparison its architecture
relies on; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses this candidate
explanation and its limits.

\subsection{Does more training diversity close the gap?}
\label{sec:diversity2}

We test whether Section~\ref{sec:pointer}'s small zero-shot improvement
depends on training diversity: with no architecture change, we apply
Section~\ref{sec:diversity1}'s per-role random alphabet selection during
training itself, instead of 6 fixed alphabets, and grow the alphabet
pool from 6 to the full 25 non-E alphabets, so every training example
draws a random set of 9 symbols from that larger collection.

Training reaches 98.41\% cell accuracy on the held-out (also randomly
mixed) validation set by epoch 19, over 20 epochs of $\sim$178{,}560
fully-represented puzzles (no repetition needed, since every epoch's
pass draws a new random assignment for each puzzle).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\footnotesize

\subsection{Disentangling pool size from mixing exposure}
\label{sec:disentangle}

Section~\ref{sec:diversity2} changed two variables relative to
Section~\ref{sec:pointer} at once: the alphabet pool grew from 6 to 25
letters, and training switched from never mixing letters within a puzzle
to always mixing them. Holding pool size fixed at Section~\ref{sec:pointer}'s
6 letters while giving it Section~\ref{sec:diversity2}'s always-mixed
training format reproduces the same tight, uniform band of mixed-alphabet
accuracy seen with 25 letters, in fact slightly better (98.5--98.7\%
versus 98.0--98.3\% cell accuracy; Appendix~\ref{app:disentangle}).
Going from 6 to 25 letters bought essentially nothing extra for this
sub-problem: the variable that matters is whether training ever exposes
the model to within-puzzle mixing at all, not how large the alphabet
pool is (full tables and per-trial figure in Appendix~\ref{app:disentangle}).

On zero-shot transfer to held-out E, this condition reaches 11.46\% cell
accuracy and 0\% exact match rate: the small positive signal seen in
Sections~\ref{sec:pointer} (14.47\%) and \ref{sec:diversity2} (15.85\%)
is essentially absent here, close to the roughly 11.1\% chance floor.
Exact match rate stays at exactly 0\% across all three conditions, so
the headline conclusion is unchanged either way, but this leaves open
whether the weak signal in the other two conditions depends on genuine
pool diversity or is simply run-to-run noise across single training runs
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

A single failure mode appears from every angle we tested, resolving into
two sub-problems with different answers.
\textbf{Recombining known symbols is solved by training diversity,
specifically by mixing exposure instead of pool size.} Sections~\ref{sec:diversity1}
and \ref{sec:pointer} showed graded, partial degradation on
recombined-alphabet puzzles under training that never mixed alphabets
within a puzzle; Section~\ref{sec:diversity2} showed that degradation
essentially vanishes once training always mixes a large pool of letters;
Section~\ref{sec:disentangle} located why, reproducing the same
near-perfect uniformity with only 6 letters as long as training always
mixes them. This part of the entanglement was, in the end, a matter of
what the training distribution exposed the model to, and a cheap
property to supply.

\textbf{Transfer to a symbol never seen at all is not solved by anything
we tried.} Neither the architectural change (Section~\ref{sec:pointer})
nor either training-diversity condition
(Sections~\ref{sec:diversity2}--\ref{sec:disentangle}) moved held-out
alphabet E's exact match rate off exactly 0\%. Cell accuracy's small
residual signal is less consistent than it first appeared, rising from
11.8\% (Section~\ref{sec:diversity1}) to 14.47\% (\ref{sec:pointer}) to
15.85\% (\ref{sec:diversity2}) but falling back to 11.46\%, close to
chance, in Section~\ref{sec:disentangle}; whether this reflects a real
dependence on pool diversity or run-to-run variation cannot be settled
with one seed per condition. Either way, nothing tried gives the model a
way to discover what an unseen symbol means well enough to produce a
single fully valid board.

The result underlying both halves is Section~\ref{sec:naive}'s
transplant control: the model's procedure reuses across equivalent
instances exactly, once told the symbol correspondence. Not the
computation but the recognition step remains bound to specific training
instances, consistent with the broader shortcut-learning literature
\citep{geirhos2020shortcut}; every intervention here either supplies
recognition capacity directly (the transplant control) or tries to
induce it indirectly, and none of the indirect routes closed the harder
half of the gap. One candidate account for the small residual signal in
Sections~\ref{sec:pointer}--\ref{sec:diversity2}, absent in
Section~\ref{sec:disentangle}: a ``do not repeat this symbol nearby''
detector needs only compare two representations for equality, not know
their content, so it could transfer to never-trained embeddings by
accident (untested, left to future interpretability work). Since no
data-side intervention touched the harder half of the gap, the most
promising next step is architectural: a mechanism constraining
computation to operate on similarity between representations by
construction, instead of leaving that as one option among others.
Abstractors \citep{altabaa2024abstractors} implement exactly this for
Transformers, and are a natural candidate for closing what training-side
changes could not.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Every model here is a small transformer trained from scratch under fully
controlled data; we make no claim about large pretrained models, whose
training history spans far more surface variation than any single
relabeling could add. This is deliberate: a large pretrained model could
appear to recognize a relabeled puzzle's equivalence through mechanisms
our setting excludes, e.g.\ chain-of-thought translation
\citep{wei2022chain}, tool use, or memorized pretraining exposure to
similar puzzles, none of which would show its own representations became
alphabet-invariant \citep{yang2025emergent}.

We deliberately study one domain, Sudoku, instead of several, and one
equivalence transform, relabeling the symbol alphabet, out of several a
puzzle could plausibly support (e.g.\ permuting rows and columns). The
pointer head (Section~\ref{sec:pointer}) has a structural blind spot: it
can only predict a token already present among a puzzle's own fixed
cells, and 0.80\% of puzzles from our generator have one that does not;
we filtered this out of training and left it in for evaluation. Most
training conditions in Section~\ref{sec:results} are a single run (the
few-shot sweep, Section~\ref{sec:diversity1}, is the exception, at 3
seeds); the residual zero-shot signal (Section~\ref{sec:discussion})
moved non-monotonically, possibly
run-to-run noise, though exact match rate stayed at exactly 0\%
regardless.

\section{Conclusion}

We provided a checkable operationalization of a question usually left
informal, whether a model's competence transfers between instances of
the same problem differing only in surface symbols, with a control
separating a true failure to transfer reasoning from the simpler fact
that some parameters were never trained. Applied to Sudoku, naive
transfer fails completely, while the transplant control recovers full
performance exactly: the model's procedure is reusable, but nothing in
ordinary training lets it recognize, on its own, that a new instance
calls for it. Recombining known symbols turned out to be a matter of
training diversity and is now essentially solved; transferring to a
symbol never encountered at all was untouched by every intervention we
tried. That split result points clearly to what comes next: an
architecture constraining its computation to operate on relations
between representations, instead of merely allowing it to.

\subsection{Mixed-alphabet trial protocol}
\label{app:trials}

Sections~\ref{sec:diversity1}, \ref{sec:pointer}, and
\ref{sec:diversity2} all evaluate the same fixed set of alphabet
recombinations, reporting only their range and mean in the main text to
keep the tables short. This section spells out exactly what each of
those 12 trials is, so every summarized number in the paper traces back
to a specific, reproducible assignment instead of an arbitrary sample.

A \emph{trial} is a single, fixed choice of which alphabet supplies
each of the 9 roles, evaluated once over the entire held-out test set
to produce one accuracy number, not a repeated statistical sample. The
12 fixed trials reused identically throughout the paper are: the
\emph{control} trial, assigning all 9 roles to alphabet A (role
$1{\to}A, 2{\to}A, \ldots, 9{\to}A$); the \emph{maximally mixed} trial,
cycling through all 6 alphabets in order and wrapping around once it
runs out of letters (role $1{\to}A, 2{\to}B, 3{\to}C, 4{\to}D,
5{\to}F, 6{\to}G, 7{\to}A, 8{\to}B, 9{\to}C$); and 10 \emph{random}
trials, each a separate, independently drawn assignment (one random
trial might assign role $1{\to}D, 2{\to}A, 3{\to}G, \ldots$, a
completely different, unrelated draw from the next). Each of the 12
assignments is evaluated separately over the entire held-out test set,
giving 12 separate accuracy numbers: the \emph{range} reports the
lowest and highest of the 10 random trials, and the \emph{mean} reports
their average.
