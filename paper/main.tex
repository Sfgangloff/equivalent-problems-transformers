\begin{abstract}
A reasoner is \emph{abstract} if it applies a general procedure to a problem's structure, indifferent to how that structure is concretely instantiated; it is \emph{concrete} if what it learned is tied to the particular instantiation trained on. This gives a testable marker: an abstract reasoner should transfer mastery of one problem instance to another. We study equivalence via alphabet renaming, and test transfer using a weight-transplant control (copying trained per-symbol weights onto the new instance without retraining) that distinguishes true reasoning failure from symbols never trained on. We instantiate this with Sudoku, where an equivalent puzzle is any relabeling of its digits. A transformer trained on one such puzzle transfers to an equivalent one no better than an untrained network, yet the transplant control recovers its original performance exactly. Reasoning, in that narrow sense, is abstract; recognizing that a new instance calls for it is not. We probe this recognition gap three ways. Freezing the shared network and adapting only a new alphabet's parameters fares worse than training an unfrozen model from scratch on the same data: prior exposure to other alphabets does not ease learning a new one. Recombining already-known symbols into unfamiliar combinations yields graded, partial success: above chance, short of the seamless transfer an abstract reasoner should show, degrading as combinations diverge from training. Replacing the model's fixed, per-alphabet output vocabulary with a mechanism that selects among a puzzle's own visible symbols helps substantially with recombination, but only marginally with transfer to a truly unseen alphabet. These results isolate a specific failure to recognize alphabet-equivalent instances as such, distinct from a general failure to reason, and show several natural interventions narrow, but do not close, this gap.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A model that has learned to solve a problem can be said to reason
\emph{abstractly} about it if the procedure it applies operates on the
problem's structure, indifferent to the particular form in which that
structure is presented. It reasons only
\emph{concretely} if what it has learned is tied to the specific surface
form it was trained on, rather than to the structure underneath. This
distinction is central to any claim that a learned system reasons rather
than merely fits patterns in its training distribution, but such claims
are frequently made without a test that could actually distinguish the
two possibilities. Benchmarks such as SCAN \citep{lake2018generalization}
and COGS \citep{kim2020cogs} test a closely related property,
generalizing to unseen \emph{combinations} of familiar components, and
have repeatedly found large gaps between in-distribution and
compositional-generalization accuracy in standard neural architectures.
We study a different, narrower notion of generalization: not new
combinations of familiar parts, but the exact same combinatorial
structure presented under a purely relabeled vocabulary, which leads to
a more basic
question: does the model recognize the relabeled instance as the
problem it already knows how to solve at all.

We propose a concrete operationalization of this distinction.
An abstract reasoner should treat two different instances of the same
underlying problem interchangeably: having mastered one, it should
transfer to solving the other, since its procedure does not depend on
which particular instantiation it happens to face. We instantiate
``different instances of the same problem'' through the simplest
transform that leaves a problem's structure completely intact while
changing its surface form entirely: renaming its symbol alphabet. Two
problems $P$ and $Q$ are \emph{equivalent} under this definition when
$Q$ is obtained from $P$ purely by relabeling which symbols represent
which underlying roles; nothing about the structure, constraints, or
solution changes, only the names used to present it.

This gives more than a single empirical test: a general recipe
applicable to any symbolic or combinatorial domain with an analogous
notion of relabeling. The recipe has two parts. First, define
equivalence via a checkable relabeling, and measure whether a model
trained on one instance transfers, unaided, to an equivalent one.
Second, use a targeted control to separate two failure modes that a naive transfer
evaluation cannot distinguish on its own. A model may fail on a new
instance either because its underlying reasoning does not generalize, or
simply because the new instance's symbols were never seen during
training, so any parameters associated with them remain at their random
initialization regardless of how good the model's reasoning otherwise
is; Sections~\ref{sec:pointer}--\ref{sec:diversity2} later test
directly whether that alone is enough to prevent any useful signal. We
isolate
the second explanation with a \emph{weight-transplant} control: after
training, we copy the parameters associated with each of $P$'s symbols
directly onto the parameters for $Q$'s corresponding symbols, using the
known-by-construction correspondence, with no further training. This
tells us how the model would perform on $Q$ if it already knew the
correspondence between the two instances' symbols: precisely the
information a naive zero-shot evaluation cannot provide on its own.

We instantiate this recipe concretely on Sudoku puzzles, whose instances
can be generated cheaply with a verified unique solution. Training a
transformer to solve Sudoku
puzzles written in one symbol alphabet, we find that it transfers to an
equivalent puzzle in a different alphabet at chance level, performance
indistinguishable from a network that was never trained at all, even
though the weight-transplant control recovers its original accuracy
\emph{exactly}. The model's
procedure does reuse across equivalent instances once it is told how
their symbols correspond; what it lacks is any mechanism for discovering
that correspondence on its own. We then probe this recognition gap along
three further axes: pretraining diversity, an architectural change
that removes a specific structural bottleneck, and training-time data
diversity at a much larger scale, and show that each narrows, but does
not close, the gap, with a further finding that training diversity
resolves the gap into two distinct sub-problems with different answers:
recombining already-known symbols is solved by sufficient diversity,
while transfer to a truly novel symbol is not.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item An operationalized, reusable methodology for testing knowledge
  transfer between equivalent instances of a symbolic problem, including
  a weight-transplant control that isolates true reasoning-transfer
  failure from the confound of never-trained parameters.
  \item Applied to Sudoku, we show naive transfer across
  alphabet-equivalent puzzles fails completely (statistically
  indistinguishable from an untrained network), while the transplant
  control recovers full performance exactly, demonstrating the failure
  is one of recognition, not of reusable computation (Section~\ref{sec:naive}).
  \item We show this recognition gap persists across two further natural
  interventions: pretraining diversity across several known alphabets,
  and a redesigned architecture that removes a specific structural
  bottleneck that prevents zero-shot generalization by construction;
  each narrows but does not close the gap (Sections~\ref{sec:diversity1}--\ref{sec:pointer}).
  \item We show training-time data diversity resolves this gap into two
  distinct sub-problems with different answers: recombining
  already-known symbols in novel combinations is essentially solved by
  sufficient training diversity, while transfer to a truly novel,
  never-seen symbol remains unsolved by every intervention tested
  (Section~\ref{sec:diversity2}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Neural Sudoku solving.} \citet{palm2018recurrent} introduced
Recurrent Relational Networks, which solve Sudoku via iterative message
passing between cell-level representations on a graph: the
architectural lineage our encoder-based model, and its optional
iterative-refinement mechanism, belong to. That work, and subsequent
neural combinatorial solvers, focus on solving accuracy within a single
fixed vocabulary; we instead hold solving accuracy on the training
vocabulary fixed near ceiling throughout, and ask what happens under a
symbol relabeling that leaves the underlying combinatorial problem
completely unchanged.

\paragraph{Compositional and systematic generalization.} SCAN
\citep{lake2018generalization} and COGS \citep{kim2020cogs} test whether
sequence models generalize to novel \emph{combinations} of familiar
primitives, and both find persistent, large gaps between in-distribution
and compositional accuracy across a range of architectures. Our question
is narrower and, in a specific sense, easier: we do not ask the model to
combine familiar parts in new ways, only to recognize the exact same
combination it was trained on, wearing a different vocabulary. That we
still find a total failure to recognize this, indistinguishable from an
untrained network, suggests the difficulty compositional-generalization
benchmarks document may in part reflect an even more basic failure to
abstract away from surface symbol identity at all.

\paragraph{Symbol binding and variable binding.} \citet{webb2021emergent}
is the closest direct precedent for our framing: their Emergent Symbol
Binding Network uses an indirection mechanism over external memory that
lets learned rules generalize near-perfectly to entirely novel filler
entities, given only a handful of examples, because its computation
operates on relations between representations rather than their
identity. \citet{altabaa2024abstractors} formalize a version of this idea
for Transformers directly, using a relational cross-attention mechanism
architecturally constrained to depend only on similarity between
representations, never their content. Our Section~\ref{sec:pointer}
pointer-based redesign is a much simpler, less architecturally
constrained step in the same direction: removing a fixed,
identity-keyed output vocabulary rather than building in an explicit
relational bottleneck. Our results (helping substantially with one
sub-problem, only marginally with the other) suggest the stronger,
more explicit mechanism of \citet{altabaa2024abstractors} may be
necessary where our simpler intervention is not sufficient (see
Section~\ref{sec:diversity2} and Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Pointer and copy mechanisms.} Our Section~\ref{sec:pointer}
architecture, which predicts a blank cell's value by selecting among the
symbols visible elsewhere in the same puzzle instance rather than
classifying into a fixed global vocabulary, is a direct application of
Pointer Networks \citep{vinyals2015pointer} and pointer-generator copy
mechanisms \citep{see2017get} to this setting. Both were originally
motivated by output spaces that vary with the input (sorting an input
sequence; naming a rare word from the source document) rather than by
the alphabet-equivalence question we study; we use the same mechanism
for a different purpose, to remove the specific structural obstacle (a
per-alphabet output vocabulary) that makes zero-shot transfer to a new
alphabet impossible by construction in the naive classifier design.

\paragraph{Symbolic mechanisms in pretrained language models.}
Contemporaneously with this direction of work, \citet{yang2025emergent}
identify an emergent symbolic architecture inside pretrained large
language models (symbol abstraction, symbolic induction, and retrieval
heads operating on abstract variables rather than raw tokens) that
supports abstract reasoning in a related sense. Our study is
complementary: rather than probing an existing large pretrained model,
we train small transformers from scratch under fully controlled data,
isolating the specific alphabet-relabeling manipulation from every other
confound a pretrained model's training history would introduce.

\paragraph{Positioning.} To our knowledge, the specific combination we
use, defining instance equivalence via a checkable symbol relabeling,
together with a weight-transplant control that isolates true
reasoning-transfer failure from the confound of never-trained
parameters, has not been used together in this form, though each piece
draws on the established ideas above.

\section{Problem Setup and Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task and equivalence}

We use Sudoku as a concrete, verifiable instance of the general setup
described in Section~1: a $9\times9$ grid, partitioned into nine
$3\times3$ boxes, must be filled with symbols such that each symbol
appears exactly once in every row, column, and box. A \emph{puzzle} is a
partial assignment (some cells given, the rest blank) with a unique
completion satisfying these constraints; a \emph{solution} is that
completion. Crucially, the constraint structure refers only to
\emph{repetition} of symbols, never to their identity: any bijective
relabeling of a valid solution's 9 symbols yields another valid
solution to the correspondingly relabeled puzzle. This is what makes
Sudoku a clean instance of our general definition: an \emph{alphabet} is
an injective map from the 9 canonical digit roles $\{1, \ldots, 9\}$ to
9 distinct token ids, and two puzzles are equivalent exactly when one is
the other's board relabeled under a different alphabet, with the blank
symbol (token 0) fixed across all alphabets.

\subsection{Data}

We generate 200{,}000 puzzles once, in a canonical alphabet (token ids
$1$--$9$), each with 40 given clues and a solution verified unique by
constraint propagation with backtracking. Every other alphabet used
anywhere in this paper is a lazy relabeling of this same base set, so
all reported comparisons across alphabets are guaranteed to be over
\emph{identical} underlying puzzles. We use a fixed 90/5/5 train/val/test
split (180{,}000 / 10{,}000 / 10{,}000 puzzles), with the same puzzle
indices reused across every alphabet.

Each alphabet is a disjoint block of 9 token ids: the $k$-th letter of
the alphabet (A, B, C, \ldots) is assigned the token-id offset $9(k-1)$,
so alphabet A is the canonical alphabet itself, B is a $+9$ shift, and so
on. One letter, which we call E, is reserved held out: it is never
included in any pretraining pool in any experiment in this paper, so it
consistently serves as the truly-unseen-symbol test throughout.

\subsection{Models}

Both architectures we study share an encoder body: a token embedding
table, additive row/column/box positional embeddings, and a standard
pre-norm Transformer encoder (8 layers, $d_{\text{model}}=384$, 8
attention heads). They differ only in how a blank cell's encoded
representation is turned into a predicted token.

\paragraph{Classifier head (baseline).} A linear layer maps each cell's
representation to $9K$ logits, one per digit per alphabet among the $K$
alphabets ever included in training, and predicts the literal token id
directly (never a ``digit within my own alphabet,'' which would presuppose
the model already knows its own alphabet's identity). This head's size
is fixed at training time: a novel alphabet's $9$ logit rows do not
exist yet and cannot be added without retraining, which is the
structural bottleneck motivating our second architecture
(Section~\ref{sec:pointer}).

\paragraph{Pointer head.} For each blank cell, we instead identify the
(at most 9) distinct token ids that appear among that specific puzzle
\emph{instance}'s own given clues, and predict which of them belongs in
the cell. A learned query, computed from the blank cell's encoded
representation, is compared via dot product against each candidate's
raw token-embedding vector (the same embedding table used on the input
side; no separate output projection exists), and the resulting scores
are softmaxed over the present candidates. The output space is bounded
by the board (at most 9 slots) regardless of how many alphabets have
ever been seen during training. A puzzle's target is only well-defined
for a blank cell if the correct digit for that cell is itself among that
puzzle's own givens, not merely somewhere in the completed grid; measured
directly on our generator, 0.80\% of puzzles violate this. We filter such
puzzles out for training, so every training target is well-defined, and
deliberately leave them in for evaluation, so reported numbers reflect
this real (if small) architectural limitation rather than hiding it.

Both architectures optionally support unrolling the shared encoder for
several iterations, feeding back each step's prediction for still-blank
cells as if it were a new given (RRN/IREM-style); all experiments in this
paper use a single pass ($T=1$).

\subsection{The weight-transplant control}

After training a model on alphabet A, we can ask how it would perform on
an equivalent puzzle in alphabet B \emph{if it already knew} the
digit-for-digit correspondence between A and B's tokens, independent of
whether B's own parameters ever received a training signal. For the
classifier head, we do this by copying, for every digit $d \in \{1,
\ldots, 9\}$, the trained embedding row and output-head row/bias for
A's token for $d$ directly onto B's token for $d$ (a plain tensor
copy, no gradient step, no retraining) before evaluating on B. (The
pointer head needs no analogous control: it never has per-alphabet
output parameters to copy in the first place, which is precisely its
motivation.) This isolates two failure modes a naive cross-alphabet
evaluation cannot distinguish on its own: failure because the model's
reasoning does not generalize across alphabets, versus failure simply
because B's parameters were never trained at all.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report \emph{cell accuracy} (fraction of blank cells filled
correctly), \emph{exact match rate} (fraction of boards filled
completely correctly), and \emph{valid rate} (fraction of boards that
are legal completions, meaning no repeated symbol in any row, column, or
box, consistent with the given clues, regardless of whether they match
one specific labeling). For the classifier head, computing cell accuracy
requires restricting the argmax to the evaluated alphabet's own 9
logits: an unrestricted argmax lets other alphabets' classes compete,
and once the transplant control makes two alphabets' rows numerically
identical for the same digit, argmax's tie-breaking would otherwise
systematically favor whichever alphabet has the lower token offset,
which does not reflect real model behavior. Valid rate is the primary metric
whenever a specific ground-truth correspondence cannot be assumed known
(Sections~\ref{sec:pointer}--\ref{sec:disentangle}): since Sudoku's
solutions are equivariant to symbol relabeling, there are $9!$ equally
valid labelings of any completed grid, so exact match against one
arbitrarily chosen labeling would unfairly penalize a model that solved
the puzzle correctly under a different, equally valid one.

\subsection{Training protocol}

All models are trained with AdamW, batch size 256, for 20 epochs unless
otherwise noted, on a single A100 GPU per run. Full hyperparameters are
listed in the Appendix.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:results}

Unless noted otherwise, every experiment below uses the data, model, and
metrics of Section~\ref{sec:method}, trained on a single A100 GPU per
run.

\subsection{Does the model discover the equivalence on its own?}
\label{sec:naive}

We train the classifier head on alphabet A only and, separately, on
alphabet B only, plus a reference model trained on the union of both.
Each single-alphabet model is evaluated three ways: on its own alphabet
(\emph{self}); on the other alphabet, unaided (\emph{naive cross}); and
on the other alphabet with the weight-transplant control applied
(\emph{transplanted cross}).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{llllr}
\hline
Model & Eval alphabet & Mode & Cell acc. & Exact match \\
\hline
A & A & self & 99.41\% & 92.2\% \\
A & B & naive cross & 11.28\% & 0\% \\
A & B & transplanted cross & 99.41\% & 92.2\% \\
B & B & self & 99.46\% & 92.3\% \\
B & A & naive cross & 10.95\% & 0\% \\
B & A & transplanted cross & 99.46\% & 92.3\% \\
random init & (either) & floor & 11.10--11.17\% & 0\% \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Naive cross-alphabet transfer lands exactly at the random-init
floor; the transplant control recovers the self-eval number exactly.}
\label{tab:naive}
\end{table}

Naive cross-alphabet transfer lands exactly at the random-init floor: not
somewhat worse than self-eval, but statistically indistinguishable from
a network that was never trained at all. The transplant control recovers
the self-eval number to four significant figures, with no retraining.
The only thing separating 11\% from 99\% is knowing the symbol
correspondence, not some deeper representational gap: the model's
solving competence is bound to the specific tokens it trained on, and it
does not spontaneously discover that a renamed puzzle is the same
problem it already knows how to solve.

\subsection{Does more alphabet diversity help?}
\label{sec:diversity1}

Experiment~\ref{sec:naive} used one alphabet per model. We ask two
further questions with a classifier pretrained on $K=6$ alphabets
(A,B,C,D,F,G; E held out), 60{,}000 puzzles per alphabet, 20 epochs
(99.57\% cell accuracy across all six by the end of training).

\paragraph{Few-shot adaptation.} For held-out E, we compare two
conditions given $n \in \{100, 1000\}$ labeled E-puzzles, 3 seeds each:
\emph{finetune} freezes the entire pretrained body and lets only E's own
embedding and output rows adapt; \emph{scratch} trains a fresh,
unfrozen model on the same small data.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\hline
Condition & $n=100$ & $n=1000$ \\
\hline
zero-shot (no fine-tuning) & 11.8\% & -- \\
finetune (frozen body) & 26.8\% & 39.5\% \\
scratch (unfrozen) & 26.5\% & 69.2\% \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Mean cell accuracy on E's held-out test split. At $n=1000$ the
finetune condition is nearly flat across all 3 seeds.}
\label{tab:fewshot}
\end{table}

At $n=100$ the two conditions are indistinguishable. At $n=1000$,
training from scratch clearly beats reusing the frozen pretrained body,
and the finetune number barely moves across seeds, a real ceiling rather
than noise. If the frozen body were a portable, alphabet-agnostic
solving procedure, reusing it and only relearning vocabulary should have
been easier than relearning everything from the same data. It was not:
the shared network body is entangled with the specific alphabets it was
trained on, not a clean, reusable abstraction.

\paragraph{Mixed-alphabet compositional test.} Using the same $K=6$
checkpoint, we recombine the 6 known alphabets instead of testing a new
one: each of the 9 digit roles independently draws its symbol from a
possibly different known alphabet, so the resulting combination was
never presented as one coherent alphabet during training, even though
every individual symbol is well learned.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\hline
Trial & Cell accuracy \\
\hline
control (all digits from alphabet A) & 99.49\% \\
maximally mixed (9 digits, 6 alphabets, cycled) & 44.5\% \\
10 random mixes & 23.2\%--70.8\% (mean 45.4\%, s.d.\ 12.4\%) \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Recombining known alphabets: well above chance, well below the

\label{tab:mixed1}
\end{table}

Accuracy is far above the roughly 11\% chance level but far below the
99\% ceiling, and it tracks how coherent the specific mix is: dominated
by one alphabet scores high, spread evenly across many scores low. Both
results point the same way: the model has partial, graded compositional
ability rather than a clean, disentangled binding of each symbol to its
meaning.

A real average improvement, and the pointer head helps most on the
classifier's worst trials: the two largest gains land on the
classifier's two lowest scores, and the floor across all trials rises
from 23.2\% to 34.4\%. But the improvement is uneven; 4 of the 11
non-control trials get worse, including the most systematic one
(maximally mixed cycle). The architecture change helps more with the
easier question (recombining known symbols) than the harder one (a
truly novel symbol), in both cases by a modest rather than decisive
margin.

One candidate explanation for why the pointer head shows any positive
zero-shot signal at all, where the classifier shows none: the
classifier's failure was total because no mechanism let gradient reach
an untrained output row. The pointer head's E-alphabet embeddings are
also untrained, at their random initialization, but every occurrence of
a given E-symbol within one puzzle uses the identical random vector, and
detecting that two positions hold the same vector is a property of
equality between representations, not of their content. A ``do not
repeat a symbol within this row, column, or box'' mechanism built on
that kind of comparison could plausibly fire correctly even for
never-trained tokens. This would predict the observed pattern (a weak
but real signal, far from a solved task) but has not been verified
directly; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses it as a candidate for
future interpretability work, not a settled finding.

\subsection{Does more training diversity close the gap?}
\label{sec:diversity2}

Section~\ref{sec:pointer} left a real but small zero-shot improvement
unexplained. Two candidate explanations motivate this experiment: the
$K=6$ pretraining alphabets were each repeated across 20 epochs, letting
the model partially memorize those 6 specific combinations; or something
more structural limits the pointer head regardless of training
diversity. We test the first explanation directly, at low cost, reusing
the pointer architecture from Section~\ref{sec:pointer} without any code
change to the model itself: instead of 6 fixed alphabets, every single
training example draws an independently random digit-to-letter mapping,
fresh, from a pool of 25 non-E letters (each of the 9 digit roles
independently, not one random coherent alphabet per puzzle). No fixed
combination is ever repeated for the model to memorize.

Training reaches 98.41\% cell accuracy on the held-out (also randomly
mixed) validation set by epoch 19, over 20 epochs of $\sim$178{,}560
fully-represented puzzles (no repetition needed, since every epoch's
pass already gives each puzzle a fresh mix).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\hline
Metric & All six known alphabets \\
\hline
Cell accuracy & 98.2\% \\
Valid rate & 76.9\%--78.0\% \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{In-distribution gate, testing each alphabet as a pure coherent
block, as in Table~\ref{tab:pointer-gate}.}
\label{tab:diversity2-gate}
\end{table}

This is notably lower than Section~\ref{sec:pointer}'s fixed-alphabet
pointer (99.3\% cell, 92.5--93.0\% valid). This model almost never saw a
pure single-alphabet puzzle during training, since each of the 9 digit
roles is drawn independently from 25 letters, so a coherent alphabet is
itself a rare, close-to-out-of-distribution case for it, unlike for the
fixed-alphabet-trained model.

The trial-to-trial variance seen in every earlier condition (classifier
23--71\%, fixed-alphabet pointer 34--76\%) has essentially vanished:
every one of the 12 trials, from the coherent control to the most
aggressively mixed random draw, now scores within a 0.3 percentage-point
band. Random-mix training exposes the model to an enormous number of
recombinations of the same 25 known letters; by the end of training it
has effectively seen every letter co-occurring with every other letter
in nearly every digit-role position, many times over, so no new
combination of already-known letters is truly novel to it anymore. But
alphabet E was never in that pool at all, so recombining other letters
injects no information into E's specific embedding, which remains
exactly as untrained as it always was. Training diversity fixes whether
the model has seen a given combination of known symbols before; it does
not fix whether the model has ever seen a given symbol at all. This
training regime also trades some peak performance (coherent-alphabet
valid rate drops from about 93\% to 77--79\%) for a large gain in
consistency across the whole combinatorial space (valid-rate range
collapses from 0--93\% to 76--80\%), an ordinary specialist-versus-
generalist trade-off.

\subsection{Disentangling pool size from mixing exposure}
\label{sec:disentangle}

Section~\ref{sec:diversity2} changed two variables relative to
Section~\ref{sec:pointer} at once: the alphabet pool grew from 6 to 25
letters, and training switched from never mixing letters within a puzzle
to always mixing them. This experiment holds pool size fixed at
Section~\ref{sec:pointer}'s level (the identical 6 letters, and an
identical vocabulary size) while giving it Section~\ref{sec:diversity2}'s
always-mixed training format, to find out which variable actually drove
the variance collapse.

On zero-shot transfer to held-out E, this condition reaches 11.46\% cell

pool diversity or is simply run-to-run noise: each condition here is a
single training run, so we flag this as a real open question rather than
a resolved finding (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

A single failure mode appears from every angle we tested, and it
resolves into two sub-problems with different answers.

\textbf{Recombining known symbols is solved by training diversity,
specifically by mixing exposure rather than pool size.}
Sections~\ref{sec:diversity1} and \ref{sec:pointer} both showed graded,
partial degradation on recombined-alphabet puzzles under training that
never mixed alphabets within a puzzle; Section~\ref{sec:diversity2}
showed that degradation essentially vanishes once training always mixes
a large pool of letters; Section~\ref{sec:disentangle} located why, by
reproducing the same near-perfect uniformity with only 6 letters, as
long as training always mixes them. This part of the entanglement was,
in the end, a matter of what the training distribution exposed the model
to, and a cheap property to supply.

\textbf{Transfer to a symbol never seen at all is not solved by anything
we tried.} Neither the architectural change in Section~\ref{sec:pointer}
nor either training-diversity condition in
Sections~\ref{sec:diversity2}--\ref{sec:disentangle} moved held-out
alphabet E's valid rate off exactly 0\%. Cell accuracy's small residual
signal is less consistent than it first appeared: it rose from 11.8\% in
Section~\ref{sec:diversity1}'s classifier baseline to 14.47\% in
Section~\ref{sec:pointer} to 15.85\% in Section~\ref{sec:diversity2}, but
fell back to 11.46\%, close to chance, in Section~\ref{sec:disentangle}.
Whether that pattern reflects a real dependence on pool diversity or
simply run-to-run variation across a narrow band is not settled by a
single run per condition; either way, valid rate never once moved, so
nothing tried so far gives the model a way to discover what an unseen
symbol means well enough to produce a single fully valid board.

The result underlying both halves of this picture is
Section~\ref{sec:naive}'s transplant control: the model's procedure
reuses across equivalent instances exactly, once it is told the symbol
correspondence. Read against the framing in Section~1, this sharpens
what ``concrete'' actually means for these models. It is not that the
computation itself fails to generalize; handed the correspondence, it
transfers perfectly. What remains bound to specific training instances
is the recognition step, the part that would let the model notice, on
its own, that a new instance calls for the procedure it already has.
Every intervention in this paper either supplies more of that
recognition capacity directly (the transplant control) or tries to
induce it indirectly through architecture or data, and only the indirect
routes were tested at scale; none closed the harder half of the gap.

One candidate account for the small residual signal in
Sections~\ref{sec:pointer} and~\ref{sec:diversity2}, and its absence in
Section~\ref{sec:disentangle}, is the vector-equality mechanism sketched
in Section~\ref{sec:pointer}: a ``do not repeat this symbol nearby''
detector that only needs two representations to be compared for
equality, not for their content to be known, could transfer to
never-trained embeddings by accident, and could plausibly depend on how
much practice the model gets comparing many distinct vectors during
training. This is consistent with the pattern observed but has not been
tested directly; confirming it would need an interpretability probe
comparing attention or compatibility patterns on held-out E's cells
against the same puzzle in a known alphabet, which we leave to future
work.

Given that no data-side intervention touched the harder half of the gap,
the most promising next step is architectural: a mechanism that
constrains computation to operate on similarity between representations
by construction, rather than leaving that as one option among others the
way the pointer head in Section~\ref{sec:pointer} does. Abstractors
\citep{altabaa2024abstractors} implement exactly this idea for
Transformers through relational cross-attention, and are a natural
candidate for closing what training-side changes could not.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Every model in this paper is a small transformer trained from scratch
under fully controlled data; we make no claim about how these findings
would look in a large pretrained model, whose training history already
spans far more surface variation than any single alphabet-relabeling
manipulation could add. Section~\ref{sec:related} discusses one recent
result suggesting a related distinction is visible inside pretrained
LLMs too \citep{yang2025emergent}, but we did not test this directly.

We deliberately study one domain, Sudoku, rather than several; this
keeps every comparison across experiments over identical underlying
puzzles, at the cost of not yet knowing how much of what we find is
specific to this domain's particular constraint structure. Within that
domain, we study one equivalence transform, relabeling the symbol
alphabet, out of several a puzzle could plausibly support (permuting
rows and columns, or changing notation, for instance); whether these
other transforms produce the same recognition gap is untested.

The pointer head (Section~\ref{sec:pointer}) has a structural blind
spot: it can only predict a digit whose token already appears among a
puzzle's own givens, and 0.80\% of puzzles from our generator have at
least one digit that does not. We measured this directly rather than
assuming it away, filtered it out of training, and left it in for
evaluation, but the architecture is mechanically unable to solve those
puzzles regardless of how well it has otherwise learned.

Most training conditions in Section~\ref{sec:results} are a single run;
the few-shot sweep in Section~\ref{sec:diversity1} is the exception, at
3 seeds. The residual zero-shot cell-accuracy signal discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} moved across a narrow, non-monotonic range
between conditions, and we cannot currently rule out that this
particular pattern is run-to-run variation rather than a real effect of
pool diversity; every condition's valid rate, our primary and more
decisive metric, is unaffected by this uncertainty, since it stayed at
exactly 0\% throughout.

\section{Conclusion}

We gave a checkable operationalization of a question usually left
informal, whether a model's competence transfers between instances of
the same problem that differ only in surface symbols, together with a
control that separates a true failure to transfer reasoning from the
simpler fact that some parameters were never trained. Applied to
Sudoku, naive transfer across a relabeled alphabet fails completely,
while the transplant control recovers full performance exactly: the
model's procedure is reusable, but nothing in ordinary training gives it
a way to recognize, on its own, that a new instance calls for it. Across
several attempts to close that gap, one sub-problem, recombining symbols
the model already knows, turned out to be a matter of training
diversity and is now essentially solved; the other, transferring to a
symbol the model has never encountered at all, was untouched by every
architectural and data-side intervention we tried. That split result is
itself the clearest signal for where to look next: an architecture that
constrains its computation to operate on relations between
representations, rather than merely allowing it to, is now the
best-motivated remaining direction.
